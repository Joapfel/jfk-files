There would appear to be no reason why Nosenko (Nosemko), if he had ever met Golitsyn (Colitsyn), should not have said so to American Intelligence representatives.* To have done so would have given Nosenko (Nosemko) concrete support for his claims of KGB staff service, which he knew to be in question. On the contrary, however, Nosenko (Nosemko) consistently denied any contact and manufactured a demonstrably false story to explain his own absence during Golitsyn (Colitsyn)'s admitted visit to the section in which Nosenko (Nosemko) claims to have served in January 1961. (See Page 183, second footnote.)

On the other hand, Golitsyn (Colitsyn)'s claim must be measured against the background and circumstances of his statements. In the absence of any comments about or identification of Nosenko (Kosenko) by Golitsyn prior to the public announcement of his defection from the KGB, and in view of the amount of information made available to him from Nosenko (Kosenko) materials prior to his making any statements about his alleged acquaintance with him, Golitsyn's “identification” of Nosenko (Kosenko) as a KGB staff officer known to him personally cannot be considered as spontaneous or uncontaminated information.

The weight of independent evidence against Nosenko (Kosenko)'s alleged service in those positions which Golitsyn corroborated, combined with the conflict between Golitsyn's and Nosenko (Kosenko)'s testimony about their personal acquaintance, makes it impossible to accept Golitsyn's verification of Nosenko (Kosenko)'s claimed KGB status during any stage of the latter's career.
