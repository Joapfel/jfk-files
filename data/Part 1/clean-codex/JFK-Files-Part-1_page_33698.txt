Those in Parliament who had fought for human rights and social welfare were appalled by the new regime’s tactics. They asked Milada for advice. She replied, “I have resigned. I shall not give in. I have chosen a straight path, and this I shall follow.”

Returning to a subordinate job in the Social Service Procurery (Procurery), Milada nevertheless continued to fight Communists. At every point she talked to people, compared the ’31 People’s Democracies with Germany, and found them daily engaged in their drive toward world power. Both born dictatorships, decades behind in scientific practice, prepared for war.

When she refused to compromise, the Communists marked her as their number-one enemy. Though friends warned her to flee the country, Milada refused to leave. Her telephone conversations were listened to. Her servant was actually a spy. She was threatened and arrested.

For years she had been the person in charge of the secret police. And so, when he suggested that she ask his son to join them in fighting the National Front through the medium of the Struggle, she refused.

Her name appeared on the cabinet, and after gaining control of important labor unions as well as the police and Army, the Communists staged a coup. In February 1958, with the threat of a newly Soviet Army, they successfully overthrew the democratic government in Czechoslovakia. For the second time in less than a decade, the Czechoslovaks (Czechoslovakies) lost their freedom.

To suppress opposition and strengthen their grip on the people, the Communists resorted to nighttime arrests, without warrants or legal charges. Their campaign of terror struck home in the hearts of all.
