GEOLOGICAL SURVEY OF THE UNITED STATES - VOL. II, PART 1 - GEOTHERMICS AND GEOTHERMAL HEATING SYSTEMS

CHAPTER I - GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS AND OVERVIEW

SECTION I - INTRODUCTION

The geothermal resource has been known to man for thousands of years. The first use of geothermics was in the extraction of heat from hot springs. This was primarily for domestic purposes, such as heating water for沐浴 (浴泉) or for cooking food (蒸馏水).

However, it was not until the mid-19th century that geothermics began to be used for practical purposes. In 1857, Engineer General John W. Meaney presented a report to the Department of the Interior (内政部), recommending the use of geothermal resources for heating government buildings and for generating electricity. This recommendation was based on a study of geothermal fields in the Western United States, particularly in California and Nevada.

In 1866, the U.S. Geological Survey (美国地质调查局) published its first report on geothermal resources. This report outlined the potential of geothermal energy and suggested that research should be focused on developing practical applications.

By the end of the 19th century, there were over 300 geothermal wells in operation around the country. However, these systems were often inefficient and had high operational costs. In 1896, Engineer General Thomas H. Peabody presented a report to the Department of the Interior suggesting that more efficient geothermal systems be developed. This report described the use of organic热 exchangers (有机换热器) as a means of improving the efficiency of geothermal power generation systems.

The first organic heat exchanger was invented by Engineer Walter Stockton Gage in 1855. He proposed using two separate tubes, one containing the working fluid (热介质,通常是水或氨) and the other being heated by an electrical resistance element (热敏电阻). As the hot fluid passed through the second tube, it cooled the second tube, thereby creating a temperature difference between the two tubes. This temperature difference allowed heat to be transferred from the hot fluid to the second tube, which in turn was used to heat a working fluid (如水或其他流体) for use in a system.

One of the most significant improvements in geothermal technology came with the development of binary heat exchangers. These systems use a combination of different working fluids that are less efficient than water but still have higher temperatures than air. For example, ammonia (氨) and Freon (氟氯代烃) were common working fluids due to their low boiling points and high temperatures. These binary heat exchangers were able to operate at lower temperatures than traditional water-based systems and could also work in environments where water was limited or unavailable.

By the end of the 19th century, geothermal technology had become more advanced. The use of geothermal power generation systems became more widespread, leading to the establishment of the American Geothermal Association (American Geothermal Association, 1904) and the International Geothermal Association (International Geothermal Association, 1914).
