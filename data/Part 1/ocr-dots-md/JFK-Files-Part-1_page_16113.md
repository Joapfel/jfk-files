The staple of the private arms trade is the aneroid battery, or the Old Red Bull. They are hardly perceptible items of caviar which sell steadily everywhere from the Conco to C Section Banda. There is, for example, the 20mm. automatic carbine, priced anywhere from $350 to $750. Delivery price will depend on condition, availability, accessibility of ammunition and shipment difficulties (open or clandestine).

Other longtime favorites are the Browning Automatic Rifle (from $95 to $300); the big-bang M1 Garand rifle ($10 to $150); the Enfield No. 1, popula- tion because of its low price (around $25): the Madsen .50 cal. machine gun ($200 to $500): the infantryman's Big Daddy: the .50 cal. Browning machine gun ($100 to $1,000), and the 57mm. recoil- less rifle, which will convert a launch into a warship at close range and in the jungle can make a guerrilla platoon's firepower equal to that of the regulars.

For all these package tests in various laboratories, their personal experience tends to be unusually old-fashioned. Perhaps because of an inner identification with adven- turers types of a by-gone past, they tend to reflect charism, eccentric turn of the century manners. This is true even of such old-style pistol packing characters as Ro- lando Masferter, who is often called the Caribbean's most feared gang land. An astonishingly literate, brilliant, erudite Cuban who was raised in the United States and graduated at the head of his law school class, Masferter has been las- ciated by violence since his first taste of it at 19, in the Spanish Civil War. He left half his left foot in Spain, but came out of the hospital an agon of the Inter- national Brigade and for years afterward lived the life of an international Commu- nist Party enforcer...

After World War II, Masferter swung to the right and became a newspaper ty-coon, senator, cattle magnate and over- land of the Caribbean's biggest gang of gentrymen. Now living in the United States, he is a colorful demonstration of the mysterious ways of monitions manipula- tion. While officially restricted to resi- dence in the State of New York, he managed to equip, in Miami, a 300 man Haitian invasion array with everything from infantry boots to a B-25 bomber. Now under indictment for this "stunt," Masferter is awaiting trial in the Miami federal court.

The last time the U.S. government tried to convict him on similar charges, he got the case thrown out of court—much like Hawke—by basing out that "I wasn't doing anything the government was not doing, too."
