<table><tr><td>AUSTRALIAN OFFICER</td></tr></table>

Prior to a trip to Germany (Subject was at the time in Paris on business) he was arrested by the German regular police. He explained to us that he had worked with a girl who acted as intermediary in certain documentation affairs. It appears that the girl was untruthfully involved with German police (It is not quite clear from Subject's story whether she worked for the Germans truthfully or untruthfully) and had passed on information concerning Subject's activities.

Be it as it may, Subject was arrested on the railroad station which had been closed-off by German police especially for this operation. He was apprehended in the presence of the girl. He had a pistol with him which complicated matters slightly.

Because the case was political, he was not held unduly by the criminal investigation branch but passed on to the Gestapo. Subject was brought to Berlin where he was interrogated for several months at Gestapo HQ. He told us that he had given them the information desired and that he had never been beaten or maltreated. He mentioned that his interrogator or interrogators changed their apparel frequently, sometimes appearing in civilian clothes, at times in navy or army uniforms.

He was questioned about the techniques employed and later was requested to consult on document forgeries; i.e. recognizing forgeries submitted to him among genuine documents. Subject told us that this was almost impossible, because his forgeries were so well made that it was hard to tell the fake from the real stuff. Nevertheless, he endeavoured to oblige and pointed out some documents of doubtful genuineness.

Later he was brought before court (it is not clear whether actually or in absentia) and given a defence lawyer. It is not clear whether he was sentenced to death, however, it might be understood from his inferences.

Subject was moved from jail to jail and from concentration camp to concentration camp. He told us that some of the time he spent in solitary and some of the time in solitary cells. Occasionally he was manacled hand and foot. Towards the end of the war he knew that executions were carried out on masse. He resorted to a little ruse de guerre and announced to the Germans that his father and he had invented a motor that could run without gasoline. He also made it known that although he was able to draw one part of this motor, the other essential part was with his father, who in turn had cached it somewhere.

Subject now that his father could not be contacted because the part of Austria he was in at the time was occupied (liberated territory). However, he was willing to draw the mechanism for his captors. It appears that the whole idea seemed attractive to the Germans and that they demonstrated great interest (this was the period of the Wunderwaffen). Subject thinks that this saved him from impending execution. Shortly afterwards he was liberated by components of the advancing US Army.

c. After WWII until Present

It might be chronologically incorrect, but at this point it should be mentioned that Subject's father decided to be repatriated. As far as Subject knows, his father is still in Soviet Georgia and might hold an engineering job. A fact is that his father had written to his (quite a while ago) and requested books on refrigeration-engineering. Subject's sister did not wish to let the father go alone, so she repatriated with him. She was accompanied by her husband, who also was a Georgian and who had been a zecher of the German armed forces. His sister's husband immediately upon arrival in the Soviet Union was sent to Siberia. His sister is believed to be working as a language professor.

Owing to the above his mother committed suicide. Subject's older brother, who had been an officer in the French Foreign Legion (decorated a couple of times) and is an engineer, went to Germany, where he got a position as municipal engineer.
