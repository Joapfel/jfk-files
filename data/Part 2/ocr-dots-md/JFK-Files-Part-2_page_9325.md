THE CATECHISM OF THE REVOLUTIONIST

The Revolutionist's Attitude Toward Himself

The revolutionist is a doomed man. He has no personal interests, affairs, sentiments, attachments, property, not even a name of his. Everything in him is absorbed by one exclusive interest, on the one passion -- the revolution.

In the very depth of his being, not merely in word but in deed, he is broken every connection with the social order and with the whole of human society, with all the laws, appearances, and generally accepted conventions and moralities of that world which he considers his ruthless master. Should he continue to live in it, it will be solely for the purpose of destroying in more surely.

The revolutionist despises every sort of doctrinairism and has reduced the peaceful scientific pursuits, leaving them to future generations. He knows only one science, the science of destruction. For this only this purpose he makes a study of mechanics, physics, chemistry, possibly medicine. For this purpose he studies day and night the laws of human beings, their characters, situation, and all conditions of the present social system in its various strata. The object is but one: The quickest possible destruction of that ignoble item.

He despises public opinion. He despises and hates the present day life of morals with all its motivations and manifestations. To him, the never side the triumph of the revolution is ethical; all that which underlies it is unethical and criminal.

The revolutionist is a doomed man. He is merciless toward the State, toward the entire system of privileged educated classes; he needs in him no mercy from them. Between him and them there is continuous irreconcilable war to the bitter end, whether it be waged openly or secretly. He must be ready to die at any moment. He must train himself to stand torture.

Rigorous towards himself, he must also be severe with others. All men, softening sentiments of kinship, friendship, love, gratitude, and honor itself, must be snuffed out in him by the one cold passion of the revolutionary cause. For him there is only one satisfaction, conclusion, and delight—the success of the revolution. Day and night we must have one thought, one aim—inexorable destruction. Striving coldly and unfairly towards this aim, he must be ready to perish himself and to destroy with his own hands everything that hinders its realization.

The nature of a real revolutionist precludes every bit of sentimentality; romanticism, of infatuation and exaltation. It precludes even personal hatred and revenge. Revolutionary passion having become a normal phenomenon, it must be combined with cold calculation.
