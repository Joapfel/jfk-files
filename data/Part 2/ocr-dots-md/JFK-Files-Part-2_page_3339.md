Convert to MarkdownWith respect to all these matters, a most important element in the policy is needed for Cuba to realize that the determination of their political and economic life is due to Castro's concerns, including his connections with International Communism. This is needed for an alternative leadership to emerge and to organize effectively for action. This is also needed further to increase Latin American opinion to the degree of the situation and for Latin American governments to come around more to the point of view that the Cuban situation is a matter of common concern, not just a U.S.-Castro quarrel. We are using all available facilities to make this time as short as possible, but our experience in other difficult situations in the Near East and Central America indicate that immediate results are not to be expected. Instead, we are going to have, we believe, to exercise restraint from taking actions which, while reflecting our own great sense of concern and urgency, would estuarially retard the process of change which we all desire.

I should point out also that, while action in the CIG at the right time may be desirable as a means of encouraging an alternative Cuban leadership, we should probably have to contend with Soviets in the United Nations. It is almost certain that the Cubans will try to anticipate or counter any U. S. approach to the CIG by going directly to the United Nations. Furthermore, actual "enforcement action" by the CIG would probably be brought up before the United Nations under Article 53 of the UN Charter even if we argued that any action taken by the CIG constituted only "recommendations" to governments. This reinforces our view that action in the CIG—which can be taken under the Charter by concurring a meeting of Foreign Ministers, as well as under the Rio Treaty and the Carlsberg Resolution—should primarily be viewed as a means of strengthening Cuban opposition to Castro's policies at the right moment rather than as a means of quenching Ciba and waiting to see what happens. It would at present be somewhat difficult to bring a case against Cuba under the Carlsberg Resolution since the latest Special National Intelligence Estimate—which we have asked to be reviewed—does not find the Cuban Government to be Communist-dominated at present. If our own Estimate does not yet substantiate the case, it would be difficult in a short time to persuade less well-informed governments that "the political institutions" of Cuba are under International Communist control or opposition.

As you are aware, our basic political policy (see 5902/1) vis-à-vis intervention as a first resort to be approved by the President when all else fails. We would be most reluctant to make or occur in such recommendation to the President until there had been a reasonable chance for all other solutions to be tried.

Thank you.
