You knew that, to the extent that weapons had been furnished dissident groups who had expressed an interest in assassinating Trujillo. And your intention here in sending this telegram was to convey to Mr. Dearborn that the Kennedy Administration wanted no further involvement in this—

Mr. Goodwin. That's right. We found—the President found out on May 13th that pursuant to the authorization that preceded his coming into office, a couple of carbines and pistols had been transferred in order to mount a major assault on a government that had lasted for thirty years, and it seemed at that point like a formidable threat to Trujillo.

He wanted to make it absolutely clear when he found out that anybody who wanted to assassinate Trujillo in the Dominican Republic was going to do it on their own. The United States would have nothing to do with it, and we would give out no more weapons. This is why he said we do not want to condone assassination or be associated with it. And that's why I wrote the telegram that I drafted in my handwriting.

Senator Tower. Well, may I ask you, then, what you interpreted the meaning of the first sentence in number 3, “Continue to inform dissident elements of U.S. support for their position?”

Mr. Goodwin. They were—their position was that Trujillo should be overthrown and replaced by a moderate government. We supported that position, as did the United States.
