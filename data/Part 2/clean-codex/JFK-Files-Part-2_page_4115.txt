The police raised the earlier was believed as alleged by Communist agents. Tokyo newsmen declined comment, but Dugan's papers reported that Dugan was engaged in undercover investigation of the flow of U.S. military equipment into the hands of the Chinese Communists.

Tokyo police officials revealed yesterday—and the U.S. Army confirmed—that they have asked Army authorities in the U.S. to question Mrs. Maud Dugan in order to exhaust all possibilities.

An Army spokesman at Camp Zama said 2nd Army Headquarters at Ft. Medicine, Mo., had been asked by Japanese police to contact Mrs. Dugan.

Some of the questions which Japanese police wanted put to Mrs. Dugan were those concerning a description of the victim, watch contents of his pockets, and clothes of Dugan when he disappeared.

Dugan disappeared Feb. 4 after leaving his wife at a Tokyo hotel.

Supporting the theory that Dugan may have committed suicide, a Tokyo University pathologist said it is “possible” for a person to drown himself without water being found in a person's lungs if the body is in an advanced stage of decomposition.

An authority revealed no water in Dugan's lungs.

Police authorities said “the cause of death has not been determined but we are fairly sure that he did not die of suffocation or poisoning.”

When Dugan said goodbye to his wife on Feb. 4, indications are that she sensed death was near when he handed her an insurance policy, saying, “If I don't return, you'll need these.”

Meanwhile, the Army spokesman said, “nothing new” has been uncovered in the case thus far.

Dugan was also fluent in Chinese and was known to have many “contacts” among Chinese living in Japan.
